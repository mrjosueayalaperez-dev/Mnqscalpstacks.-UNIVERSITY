Phase I — Market Foundations · Module 6 of 8

Risk Management & Position Sizing

Learning Objective

By the end of this module, you can define your risk per trade in dollars and as a fraction of an account, compute position size from a stop distance using MNQ's $2-per-point value, explain why the stop is chosen before the entry — and state the non-negotiable rules, including: never risk money needed for living.

Explanation

Risk first, always

Every sound trade idea begins with the same question: "Where am I wrong?" The answer to that question is your stop — the price at which the idea is proven wrong and you exit. Here is the discipline this university drills into every student: the stop is chosen before the entry, always. If you cannot point to where you are wrong, you do not have a trade. You have a hope. And we don't gamble on hope.

This ordering matters because it reverses the beginner's instinct. Beginners pick an entry, feel excited, and only afterward wonder about the stop — by which point hope is already doing the thinking. Professionals decide the exit first. The stop is not pessimism. It is the plan.

Risk per trade: your ceiling

Risk per trade is the maximum you allow yourself to lose on a single idea — stated in dollars, and often as a small fraction of the account. Many educators use an illustrative example of 1% of the account per trade. Understand what this number is: a ceiling, not a target. You are not trying to lose 1%; you are ensuring that no single idea can ever hurt you badly.

Why a small fraction? Because losing streaks are normal. Even careful, disciplined processes lose several times in a row — that is the mathematics of uncertainty, not a personal failure. If each loss is a small fraction, a streak is a bad week. If each loss is a large fraction, a streak is the end of the account. Risk management is what lets you survive long enough for your learning to compound. Risk before reward — it is a principle because it is arithmetic.

Position sizing: the formula

Once you know your risk in dollars and your stop distance in points, the number of contracts is not a guess and not a feeling. It is a formula:

contracts = risk dollars ÷ (stop distance in points × dollars per point)

On MNQ, dollars per point is $2. So a 25-point stop on one MNQ contract risks 25 × $2 = $50. If your risk ceiling is $50, the formula gives you exactly one contract. The math decides the size; your feelings don't get a vote. That is the whole point.

This is also the deepest reason micros exist for learners: the same formula, the same market, the same lesson — at one-tenth the size. Position sizing is not about being timid. It is about being exact.

When the math says no

Sometimes the formula returns a fraction of a contract — meaning the setup needs a wider stop than your risk allows. Beginners hear "no" as a problem to solve by widening their risk. Disciplined students hear "no" as a complete answer: skip the idea, or wait for a setup whose stop fits the math. Passing on a trade is not missing out. It is risk management working exactly as designed.

Round down — and leave room for fees

Two details the formula alone doesn't show you. First: always round down to a whole contract, never up. If the math gives 1.7 contracts, you trade 1 — because 2 would spend risk you don't have. Contracts come in whole numbers; your risk ceiling is a ceiling, not a suggestion. Second: the stop is not your only cost. Every round trip carries a commission — a few dollars per contract, varying by broker — and it lands on top of whatever the stop risks. Size so that (contracts × stop distance × dollars per point) + (contracts × round-trip fee) stays inside your ceiling. If adding the fee breaks the ceiling, drop one more contract. Never assume fees are zero. And one honesty note beyond fees: slippage can make the real loss bigger than the planned stop. In a fast market your stop order can fill worse than its trigger price — the formula plans the risk, but the market decides the fill. That is one more reason the ceiling is a ceiling: plan for the worst fill, not the printed one.

Non-Negotiable Rules
  • Never trade money needed for living. Rent, food, bills, and family money are never risk capital. If losing it would change your life, it does not belong in a trading account.
  • Never move a stop because of hope. The stop is the plan. Moving it when price approaches is quitting the plan at the exact moment the plan matters most.
  • Never revenge trade. After a loss, the next trade is not "the one that gets it back." There is no such trade. There is only the next independent idea, taken at the right size or not at all.
  • Never trade on memorized margin numbers. Requirements change. Always check current requirements with your provider.

Worked Example

Worked Example — Sizing a Trade on MNQ

The setup (illustrative): a student paper-trades with an illustrative account of $5,000 and a risk ceiling of 1% = $50 per trade.

Case 1: the idea needs a 25-point stop (the chart structure says the idea is wrong 25 points away).

contracts = $50 ÷ (25 × $2) = $50 ÷ $50 = 1 contract

Price risk is $50 (1 × 25 pts × $2). Adding an illustrative $1.50 round-trip fee brings total risk to $51.50, which is over the $50 ceiling. By the rule above, drop to 0 contracts: pass on this trade or wait for a setup with a stop under 25 points (at 24 pts, $48 + $1.50 = $49.50, which fits). Round down, and never round up to squeeze it in.

Fee is illustrative; check your broker's current MNQ round-trip cost.

Case 2: a different idea needs a 50-point stop.

contracts = $50 ÷ (50 × $2) = $50 ÷ $100 = 0.5 contracts

Half a contract doesn't exist. The math says no — the setup is too big for this risk ceiling. The disciplined student skips it or waits for a tighter setup. "No" is a complete answer, and honoring it is what separates students from gamblers.

Account size, risk percentage, and all figures are illustrative teaching numbers — not recommendations, not market data, and not promises of any outcome.

Practice Exercise

  1. Run the formula three times. With an illustrative $50 risk ceiling, compute position size on MNQ ($2/point) for stop distances of 10, 25, and 40 points. Round down to whole contracts, and note where the fee buffer from the lesson above would change your answer. Write each answer by hand.
  2. Compare contracts. Repeat the exercise on MES ($5/point). Notice how the contract counts differ — same math, different volume knob.
  3. Write your risk rules. In your journal, define: your maximum risk per trade, your maximum risk per day, and exactly what you will do after three consecutive losses (hint: the answer involves stepping away, not sizing up).
  4. Face the streak. Journal prompt: "If my stop were hit five times in a row, what would my account look like, and what would I do next?" Answer with numbers and with a plan.

Knowledge Check

1. What is the position-sizing formula, and what does each part mean?

Answer: contracts = risk dollars ÷ (stop distance in points × dollars per point). Risk dollars is your per-trade ceiling; stop distance is how far the idea can go against you before it's proven wrong; dollars per point is the contract's point value ($2 for MNQ). The formula converts your risk into an exact size.

2. Why must the stop be chosen before the entry?

Answer: Because "where am I wrong?" is the foundation the whole idea stands on. Choosing the entry first lets excitement and hope do the thinking; choosing the stop first makes the risk exact before any commitment exists. No defined stop means no trade — only a hope.

3. What is a risk ceiling, and why is it a ceiling rather than a target?

Answer: A risk ceiling (e.g., an illustrative 1% of the account) is the maximum allowed loss on one idea. It's a ceiling because you're not aiming to lose it — you're ensuring no single idea can seriously damage the account, so that normal losing streaks remain survivable.

4. The formula gives you 0.5 contracts. What do you do?

Answer: You pass. Half a contract doesn't exist, which means the setup's stop is too wide for your risk ceiling. You skip the idea or wait for one that fits the math — "no" is a complete answer and honoring it is discipline.

5. Name two non-negotiable rules from this module and explain why each exists.

Answer: Any two of: never risk living money (a loss must never change your life); never move a stop on hope (the stop is the plan — moving it is abandoning it under pressure); never revenge trade (no trade "gets back" a loss; each idea stands alone); never trust memorized margin numbers (requirements change — check current figures with your provider).

Risk Reminder

This module is the most important one in Phase I. Every other module teaches you to see; this one teaches you to survive.

We do not promise profits. No position-sizing formula creates an edge — it only controls how much an idea can cost you. Master this math in simulation until it is automatic, because in real markets there is no pause button while you do arithmetic under pressure.
