Phase I — Market Foundations · Module 8 of 8

Simulation Practice & Quizzes

Learning Objective

By the end of this module, you can run a structured paper-trading practice routine, keep a complete trade journal — date, setup, entry, stop, target, outcome, lesson — and self-grade a practice week with a checklist. This module contains zero real-money steps: simulation only, from first page to last.

Explanation

The laboratory

Everything you learned in Modules 1–7 was theory. Simulation — paper trading — is where theory becomes skill. A simulator lets you practice entries, stops, targets, and position sizing with zero dollars at risk. Every mistake costs you nothing but teaches you everything, if you record it honestly. That "if" is the whole module: unstructured practice builds habits, but only structured practice builds skill.

Use any free paper-trading simulator or chart replay tool. The platform doesn't matter — the routine does. M&Q University's own free simulator (simulated data only, no real money, no live orders) is inside the Teaching Avatar, in English and Español.

The practice routine

Every practice session follows the same shape. Same shape, every time — routine is what turns repetition into learning:

  1. Set the session. Pick one market (MNQ), one chart, one timeframe. Decide the day's focus — one skill, e.g., "mark structure before anything else" or "wait for the retest, no chasing."
  2. Warm up. Run your pre-session routine from Module 7: review your rules, set paper risk limits, define your "done" conditions.
  3. Paper-trade a fixed number of setups. Three is plenty. Quality over quantity — the simulator will still be there tomorrow.
  4. Journal every setup immediately. Not later, not "from memory tonight." Immediately, while the reasons are fresh.
  5. Stop at "done." Session ends when your pre-defined conditions are met — setups completed, time elapsed, or paper loss limit reached. Done is done.

The journal: where learning compounds

A journal is not a diary of feelings. It is a dataset about yourself — and datasets only work when every row has the same columns. Every paper trade gets one row with these seven columns:

  • Date — when it happened.
  • Setup — the name of the idea in your own words ("retest of 18,600 zone," "VWAP cross with RSI stretched").
  • Entry — the price you entered (paper).
  • Stop — where the idea was proven wrong (chosen before the entry, per Module 6).
  • Target — where you planned to exit with a gain.
  • Outcome — the result in points (and paper dollars if you like the math practice).
  • Lesson — one sentence. What did this trade teach you? ("Waiting for the retest kept me out of the chop." "I moved my stop — that was quitting the plan.")

Plus one honest yes/no column the pros consider the most important of all: Followed plan? After twenty rows, sort by that column. The pattern in your behavior will teach you more than any pattern in the market ever could.

Review cadence

  • End of day — 10 minutes. Read today's rows. Circle your best decision and your worst impulse. Write tomorrow's single focus.
  • End of week — 30 minutes. Read the whole week. List your three most repeated behaviors — good and bad. Pick one habit to fix next week. Just one; students who try to fix everything fix nothing.

The self-grading checklist

Each week, grade yourself honestly — this is a quiz you cannot cheat, because the only student you're grading is you:

Weekly Self-Grade
  • Rules followed: I followed my written plan on every paper trade (count the "No" rows — fewer is better).
  • Risk respected: every stop was chosen before entry; position sizes matched the formula; no stop was ever moved on hope.
  • Journal complete: every setup journaled immediately, all seven columns filled, lesson sentence written.
  • Emotions noted: I wrote down what I felt before/during/after — especially impatience, fear, and overconfidence.
  • Lessons extracted: my weekly review names one repeated behavior and one habit to fix next week.

Graduation: what "done" means

Phase I graduation is not a profit target — we do not promise profits, and we do not measure students by them. The standard is consistency of process: 20+ journaled paper trades with honest "followed plan?" answers, weekly reviews completed, and a checklist you can defend out loud. When your journal shows you follow your rules even on losing days, you have built the foundation everything else stands on.

What comes next: Phase II deepens the tools — volume, advanced structure, and the beginnings of strategy thinking. Your job between now and then is practice, journaling, and patience. No real money is discussed, suggested, or needed — not in this module, not in this phase.

Practice Exercise

  1. Build your journal. On paper or in a spreadsheet, create the seven columns: date, setup, entry, stop, target, outcome, lesson — plus the "followed plan?" yes/no column. This is now your most important trading tool.
  2. Paper-trade five sessions. Maximum three setups per session, journaling each one immediately. No session runs past your pre-defined "done" conditions. Five sessions gives you at most 15 journaled trades — graduation needs 20+, so continue into a second week until your journal clears the bar.
  3. Do one weekly review. Read all five sessions. List your three most repeated behaviors — good and bad. Choose one habit to fix next week.
  4. Grade yourself. Run the weekly self-grading checklist honestly. Write your scores down and keep them — in a month, you'll have a graph of your discipline, which is the only equity curve that matters in Phase I.

Knowledge Check

1. What are the seven journal columns, and why must every row use the same ones?

Answer: Date, setup, entry, stop, target, outcome, lesson (plus "followed plan?" yes/no). Identical columns turn the journal into a dataset about your behavior — you can only find patterns in your habits if every row is recorded the same way.

2. Why does simulation come before anything involving real money?

Answer: Because simulation is the laboratory: it lets you test every concept from Modules 1–7 — entries, stops, sizing, discipline — with zero dollars at risk. Mistakes in simulation cost nothing and teach everything; the same mistakes with real capital can end the education permanently. (Our principle: Practice Before Performance.)

3. What is the review cadence, and what does each review produce?

Answer: 10 minutes at end of day (circle best decision and worst impulse; set tomorrow's focus) and 30 minutes at end of week (list three most repeated behaviors; choose one habit to fix). Reviews convert raw journal rows into actual learning.

4. What does the weekly self-grading checklist measure?

Answer: Process, not profit: rules followed, risk respected (stops before entries, formula sizing, no moved stops), journal completeness, emotions noted, and lessons extracted. It's a quiz on discipline — the only subject Phase I grades.

5. What does "graduation" from Phase I mean?

Answer: Consistency of process — 20+ honestly journaled paper trades, completed weekly reviews, and a checklist you can defend — not profit. We do not promise profits and we do not measure students by them; graduation means your journal proves you follow your rules even on losing days.

Risk Reminder

Simulation success predicts nothing about real-money results — emotions change when dollars are real, which is exactly why the journal and the discipline come first.

We do not promise profits. There are no real-money steps in this module, this phase, or this university's foundations. If anyone ever tells you you're "ready" because your simulator looks good, remember: readiness is a process you demonstrate for months, not a feeling you get from a green week.
